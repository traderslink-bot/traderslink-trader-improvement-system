# Slippage

Slippage is the difference between the price a trader expected and the price where the order actually filled. Traders review slippage because it can affect entries, exits, risk, and realized results.

Slippage can happen in fast markets, low-liquidity stocks, wide spreads, market orders, news events, and volatile price moves.

## Simple Definition

Slippage happens when the fill price is different from the expected price.

For example:

1. A trader expects to buy at $2.00.
2. The order fills at $2.05.
3. The slippage is $0.05 per share.

Slippage can happen on entries and exits.

## Why Slippage Matters

Slippage matters because it can change the real risk-reward of a trade.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They may plan risk based on one price, but the actual fill makes the trade more expensive or riskier.

A useful review asks:

1. What price did the trader expect?
2. What price actually filled?
3. Was the spread wide?
4. Was liquidity thin?
5. Was a market order used?
6. Did slippage affect the trade result?

Slippage is part of execution quality.

## Example

A trader places a market buy order during a fast breakout. The stock is moving quickly, and the expected entry is $2.00. The fill comes in at $2.08.

That $0.08 difference may change the stop distance, position risk, and trade management plan.

## How To Review Slippage

A good slippage review looks at conditions and order behavior.

Useful questions include:

1. Was the stock liquid enough?
2. Was the spread wide?
3. Was the order type appropriate?
4. Did price move quickly during the order?
5. Was position size too large for liquidity?
6. Did slippage repeat across similar trades?

The goal is to understand whether slippage was normal market behavior or avoidable execution risk.

## FAQ

### What is slippage in trading?

Slippage is the difference between the expected order price and the actual fill price.

### Why does slippage happen?

It can happen because of volatility, low liquidity, wide spreads, fast price movement, or order type.

### Is slippage always bad?

Not always. Slippage can sometimes be favorable, but traders usually focus on negative slippage because it increases cost or risk.

### Do market orders cause slippage?

Market orders can experience slippage, especially in fast or illiquid stocks.

### Can limit orders reduce slippage?

Limit orders can control the maximum or minimum acceptable price, but they may not fill.
