# Reverse Split

A reverse split reduces the number of shares outstanding while increasing the share price proportionally. Companies often use reverse splits to meet exchange listing requirements, adjust share structure, or raise the nominal stock price.

A reverse split does not create business value by itself. It changes the share count and price math, but traders still need to review company fundamentals, dilution risk, and market reaction.

## Simple Definition

A reverse split combines shares into fewer shares.

For example:

1. A 1-for-10 reverse split turns 10 shares into 1 share.
2. A $0.50 stock may become a $5.00 stock after adjustment.
3. The total position value is intended to stay similar before market reaction.

The share price changes mechanically, but the market can still react positively or negatively.

## Why Reverse Splits Matter

Reverse splits matter because they often appear in small-cap and low-priced stocks where listing compliance, dilution, or weak price action may already be concerns.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They may see a higher post-split price and assume the company improved, even though the split only changed the share structure.

A useful review asks:

1. What was the split ratio?
2. Why did the company do the reverse split?
3. Was it related to compliance?
4. Did shares outstanding adjust as expected?
5. Is there dilution risk after the split?
6. How did price react after the split?

Reverse split review should include filings and share structure context.

## Example

A company trading at $0.20 announces a 1-for-20 reverse split. After the split, the adjusted price may be around $4.00, while the number of shares is reduced.

If the company later raises money or issues new shares, traders should review dilution risk after the split.

## How To Review A Reverse Split

A good reverse split review looks at reason, timing, and post-split risk.

Useful questions include:

1. Was the reverse split approved by shareholders?
2. Was it needed for NASDAQ or NYSE compliance?
3. What was the ratio?
4. Did the company have an active shelf or offering risk?
5. Did liquidity change after the split?
6. Did the stock hold post-split levels?

The goal is to understand whether the reverse split changed the trading risk.

## FAQ

### What is a reverse split?

A reverse split combines shares into fewer shares and raises the share price proportionally.

### Does a reverse split make a company more valuable?

No. A reverse split changes share count and price math, but it does not create business value by itself.

### Why do companies do reverse splits?

Companies may do them for exchange compliance, share structure adjustments, or to increase the nominal share price.

### Is a reverse split bad for traders?

Not always, but it can signal risk, especially if followed by dilution or weak price action.

### Does a reverse split reduce dilution risk?

Not necessarily. Some companies may raise capital after a reverse split, so filings still matter.
