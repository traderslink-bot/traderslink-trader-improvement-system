# How to Review Your Trades

To review your trades properly, do not look only at profit and loss. A useful trade review should study the full decision process: why you entered, how you managed the position, where you added or reduced, why you exited, and whether the same behavior keeps repeating.

The goal of trade review is not to beat yourself up after a loss or celebrate after a win. The goal is to understand how you traded so you can make better decisions in future sessions.

## Direct Answer

A strong trade review process should cover:

- The trade idea.
- The entry.
- The exit.
- Adds and reductions.
- Risk management.
- Trade management.
- Time of day.
- Market context.
- Mistakes or rule breaks.
- What you would repeat or avoid next time.

The best review is specific. Instead of writing, "bad trade," write what made it bad. Was the entry late? Did you add into weakness? Did you ignore a support or resistance level? Did you hold after the idea failed? Did you cut a winner too early?

## Why This Matters

Many traders judge trades too quickly.

A green trade is not always a good trade. A red trade is not always a bad trade. A trade can make money because the market bailed you out. A trade can lose money even though the entry, risk, and exit were reasonable.

Trade review helps separate outcome from process.

That matters because improvement usually comes from finding repeated patterns. One bad trade may not mean much. The same mistake showing up ten times is worth studying.

## Step-By-Step Review Process

### 1. Record the basic trade details

Start with the facts:

- Ticker.
- Date.
- Entry price.
- Exit price.
- Position size.
- Time of entry.
- Time of exit.
- Realized gain or loss.
- Notes about the original idea.

Do not rely only on memory. If possible, use broker execution data so the trade sequence is accurate.

### 2. Review the trade idea

Ask why the trade was taken.

Was it based on a breakout, pullback, support area, news catalyst, momentum, reversal attempt, or another setup? Was there a clear reason before entry, or did the trade happen because price was moving quickly?

A weak trade idea often leads to weak trade management.

### 3. Review the entry

Look at the first execution.

Ask:

- Was the entry planned?
- Was it late?
- Was it near support or directly under resistance?
- Was the stock already extended?
- Did you enter because of a real setup or because of fear of missing out?

The entry affects the rest of the trade. A poor entry can make even a decent idea harder to manage.

### 4. Review the management

This is where many traders find the most useful lessons.

Study what happened after entry:

- Did you add?
- Did you reduce?
- Did you average down?
- Did you protect open profit?
- Did you hold after the trade changed?
- Did you re-enter after exiting?

Trade management is often where repeated habits show up.

### 5. Review the exit

Ask why the trade ended.

Did the exit follow the plan, or was it emotional? Did you exit into support? Did you hold through weakness? Did you sell too early before the trade had room? Did you wait too long after the setup failed?

A good exit review studies both early exits and late exits.

### 6. Identify the lesson

End the review with one clear lesson.

Examples:

- "I entered too late after the breakout was already extended."
- "I added after the trade moved against me without a strong reason."
- "I reduced risk quickly when the trade failed, which was good process."
- "I gave back open profit because I had no exit plan."

A clear lesson is more useful than a long emotional note.

## Common Mistakes

Common trade review mistakes include:

- Reviewing only P&L.
- Writing vague notes like "bad trade."
- Ignoring entries and focusing only on exits.
- Ignoring adds and reductions.
- Failing to review winning trades.
- Blaming the market instead of reviewing decisions.
- Reviewing one trade but never looking for repeated patterns.

The best reviews are honest, specific, and repeatable.

## How A Trading Journal Helps

A trading journal gives your trade review a structure.

A strong journal should help you track more than the final result. It should help organize execution data, trade notes, mistakes, timing, and management behavior.

For active traders, manual notes can miss important details. Broker execution data can make the review more accurate because it shows what actually happened inside the trade.

## FAQ

### What is the best way to review a trade?

The best way to review a trade is to study the full process: trade idea, entry, management, exit, risk, timing, and mistakes. Do not review only the final profit or loss.

### Should I review winning trades too?

Yes. Winning trades can still include poor decisions. Reviewing winners helps you understand whether the trade was managed well or whether the result hid a weak process.

### What should I write in a trading journal?

A trading journal should include basic trade details, execution data, the trade idea, entry notes, exit notes, management notes, mistakes, lessons, and any repeated behavior you notice.

### How often should I review my trades?

Many active traders benefit from reviewing trades after each session and then doing a deeper weekly review to look for repeated patterns.

### Why is P&L not enough for trade review?

P&L shows the result, but it does not explain whether the trade was well executed. Trade review helps study the process behind the result.
