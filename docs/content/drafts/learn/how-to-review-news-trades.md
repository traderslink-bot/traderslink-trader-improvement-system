# How To Review News Trades

Learning how to review news trades can help traders understand whether a trade was based on a real catalyst, a clean setup, and a defined plan, or whether it was just a reaction to a headline. News can create fast movement, but the trade still needs structure.

A news trade should not be judged only by profit or loss. The better review looks at the catalyst, the details behind the news, the chart reaction, volume, liquidity, risk, and how the trade was managed.

## Quick Definition

Reviewing a news trade means looking back at a trade that was connected to a company announcement, filing, press release, earnings report, FDA update, contract, partnership, merger, or other catalyst.

A good review asks:

1. What was the news?
2. Was the catalyst specific or vague?
3. Did the stock already move before entry?
4. Did volume confirm real attention?
5. Did price hold key levels or fade?
6. Was risk defined before entry?
7. Was the exit based on the plan or emotion?
8. What should be improved next time?

This is not about proving the trade was good or bad because of the outcome. It is about understanding the decision-making process.

## Why It Matters To Traders

News trades can be emotional. A stock can hit scanners, spike fast, and create pressure to act before the details are clear.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They may enter because the headline sounds exciting, then later realize the news lacked financial terms, had dilution risk, or was already expected.

A structured review helps separate:

1. Strong catalyst trades.
2. Weak headline chases.
3. Late entries after the move was extended.
4. Good ideas with poor execution.
5. Bad ideas that happened to work.
6. Trades where risk was not clear.
7. Trades where the exit plan was ignored.

That kind of review is how traders can learn from the trade instead of only reacting to the P&L.

## How It Works

A useful news trade review usually has four parts.

### 1. Review The Catalyst

Start with the actual news. Read the full release, filing, or report. Identify what the company announced and whether the details supported the headline.

Ask:

1. Was the news new?
2. Was it specific?
3. Were numbers, names, dates, or terms included?
4. Was there risk language?
5. Did related filings add important context?

### 2. Review The Chart Reaction

Next, review how the stock reacted. A strong headline with weak price action may be telling you something.

Ask:

1. Did the stock gap up, spike, fade, or consolidate?
2. Did volume expand?
3. Did price hold premarket highs, VWAP, support, or resistance?
4. Did the move fail after the first reaction?

### 3. Review Your Execution

Then look at your entry, adds, exits, and trade management.

Ask:

1. Did you enter before reading enough?
2. Was the entry close to a level or late in the move?
3. Did you chase because of FOMO?
4. Did you reduce risk when the setup changed?
5. Did you follow your plan?

### 4. Review The Lesson

Finally, write the actual lesson. Keep it specific enough that it can help you next time.

Instead of writing, "bad trade," write something like: "I entered after the third candle of a news spike without checking whether the release had financial terms."

## Example Scenario

A company announces a partnership before the open. The stock gaps up and volume spikes. You enter after the stock breaks the premarket high, but it quickly fails and fades below VWAP.

A useful review would ask:

1. Did the partnership include a named partner?
2. Were financial terms disclosed?
3. Was the stock already extended from premarket lows?
4. Did the breakout hold for more than one candle?
5. Did volume increase or fade during the breakout attempt?
6. Did the stop or exit plan make sense before entry?
7. Did you stay in after the setup failed?

This kind of review turns the trade into a lesson instead of just a red or green result.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is reviewing only the headline. The headline may explain why the stock moved, but the details explain whether the catalyst had substance.

Another mistake is ignoring timing. A news trade taken in premarket, at the open, midday, or after hours can have very different liquidity and risk.

Traders also get into trouble when they judge the trade only by profit or loss. A profitable chase can still be a poor process. A losing trade can still be a well-planned trade that did not work.

A fourth mistake is not recording the lesson quickly. News trades move fast, and important details can be forgotten after the session.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

A trading journal should make the review specific, not just emotional.

Useful fields include:

1. Ticker.
2. News headline.
3. Catalyst category.
4. Source of the news.
5. Release time.
6. Key details from the news.
7. Related filings or risk notes.
8. Volume and liquidity context.
9. Key levels before entry.
10. Entry reason.
11. Exit reason.
12. What worked.
13. What failed.
14. One rule or reminder for next time.

Over time, this can help traders see whether they perform better with certain catalyst types and worse with others.

## FAQ

### What is a news trade?

A news trade is a trade connected to a catalyst such as a press release, filing, earnings report, FDA update, contract, partnership, or other company announcement.

### How should traders review news trades?

Traders can review the catalyst quality, release timing, volume, chart reaction, key levels, execution, risk, exit, and whether the trade followed a plan.

### Should a news trade be judged only by profit or loss?

No. Profit or loss matters, but it does not tell the full story. A good review looks at decision quality and trade management.

### What makes a news catalyst stronger?

A stronger catalyst often includes specific details, named parties, financial terms, clear timelines, and market relevance. Context still matters.

### Why do news trades fail?

News trades can fail because the headline is weak, the details disappoint, the move is overextended, volume fades, or price rejects key levels.

### Can a trading journal help with news trades?

Yes. A journal can help traders identify which catalysts they handle well, where they chase, and whether their news trades are planned or emotional.
