# Reverse Split vs Dilution

Reverse split vs dilution is an important distinction for traders. A reverse split reduces the number of shares and raises the price per share proportionally. Dilution increases the number of shares or potential shares, reducing existing shareholders' ownership percentage.

A reverse split is not the same thing as dilution. But traders often connect them because some companies reverse split first and later raise money, or reverse split to regain exchange compliance before future financing.

## Quick Comparison

A reverse split changes the share count and price mechanically. Dilution changes ownership percentage by adding more shares or potential shares.

Simple comparison:

1. Reverse split: fewer shares, higher price per share.
2. Dilution: more shares or potential shares.
3. Reverse split: does not raise cash by itself.
4. Dilution: often happens through financing or conversion.
5. Reverse split: ownership percentage usually stays similar immediately.
6. Dilution: existing ownership percentage can decrease.

The two are different, but both can affect how traders view share structure.

## Why Traders Connect Them

Traders connect reverse splits and dilution because they often appear in the same small-cap risk cycle.

This is where traders can get into trouble. A company may reverse split to increase its stock price and regain exchange compliance. After that, it may have more room to raise capital through offerings or other securities.

A careful review asks:

1. Why is the reverse split happening?
2. Is the company trying to regain compliance?
3. Does the company have low cash?
4. Is there an active shelf registration?
5. Are warrants or convertibles outstanding?
6. Has the company raised money after reverse splits before?
7. Did authorized shares change?
8. What does the fully diluted share count look like?

The reverse split is the corporate action. Dilution risk is the financing and share issuance context.

## How A Reverse Split Works

In a reverse split, shares are combined according to a ratio.

For example, in a 1-for-10 reverse split:

1. A holder with 1,000 shares would have 100 shares after the split.
2. A $0.20 stock price would adjust to about $2.00.
3. The market value is meant to remain roughly the same before trading changes.

A reverse split does not create cash for the company by itself. It mainly changes the share count and quoted price.

## How Dilution Works

Dilution happens when new shares are issued or securities convert into shares.

Dilution can come from:

1. Public offerings.
2. Private placements.
3. At-the-market offerings.
4. Warrants.
5. Convertible notes.
6. Preferred stock.
7. Employee stock plans.
8. Merger share issuance.

Dilution can reduce existing shareholders' ownership percentage because more shares exist or may exist later.

## Example Scenario

A company trades below $1.00 and receives a Nasdaq compliance notice. It asks shareholders to approve a reverse split. The company also has low cash and an active shelf registration.

A stronger review asks:

1. Is the reverse split mainly for compliance?
2. Does the company need cash after the split?
3. Could the higher post-split price make an offering easier?
4. Are there warrants or convertibles that adjust after the split?
5. Did the company increase authorized shares?
6. What happened after past reverse splits in similar companies?

This does not mean dilution is guaranteed. It means traders should review the full share structure.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is saying a reverse split itself is dilution. It usually is not.

Another mistake is assuming a reverse split fixes a weak company. It may fix the quoted price, but it does not automatically fix cash, operations, or financing risk.

Traders also get into trouble when they ignore what happens after the reverse split. Financing can still happen later.

A fourth mistake is failing to adjust charts and share counts after the split.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

A trading journal can help compare reverse split mechanics with dilution risk.

Useful review notes include:

1. Reverse split ratio.
2. Reason for the split.
3. Compliance status.
4. Cash position.
5. Active shelf or ATM.
6. Warrants and convertibles.
7. Authorized shares.
8. Post-split offering activity.
9. Whether the trade confused split mechanics with dilution.

Over time, this can help traders review share structure more accurately.

## FAQ

### Is a reverse split the same as dilution?

No. A reverse split reduces share count and raises price per share proportionally. Dilution adds shares or potential shares.

### Can a reverse split lead to dilution later?

It can be followed by dilution if the company later issues shares or securities, but the reverse split itself does not guarantee dilution.

### Why do small-cap companies do reverse splits?

Common reasons include regaining exchange compliance, increasing the quoted share price, or meeting listing requirements.

### Does a reverse split raise money?

No. A reverse split does not raise cash by itself.

### Why do traders watch offerings after reverse splits?

A higher post-split price can sometimes make future financing easier, especially if the company needs cash.

### What should traders review?

Traders should review cash, active shelf registrations, warrants, convertibles, authorized shares, compliance notices, and post-split financing history.
