# Trading Slippage: Why Fill Price Can Be Worse Than Expected

Trading slippage happens when an order fills at a different price than expected. For active traders, slippage can turn a good-looking setup into a worse trade because the entry or exit is not where the plan assumed.

Slippage is common in fast-moving, low-liquidity, wide-spread, or volatile stocks.

## Quick Definition

Trading slippage is the difference between the expected order price and the actual fill price.

Slippage can happen on entries and exits.

For example, if a trader expects to buy near $2.00 but fills at $2.08, the extra $0.08 is slippage. On a low-priced stock, that can be a meaningful difference.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Slippage matters because trading performance is based on actual fills, not ideal chart prices.

A trader may plan a clean risk level, but if the entry fills too high or the exit fills too low, the real risk changes. That can affect position sizing, stop distance, and profit potential.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They review the chart as if they entered perfectly, but the execution tells a different story.

## How It Works

Slippage often happens when the market moves before the order is filled or when there is not enough liquidity at the expected price.

Common causes include:

- Wide bid-ask spreads.
- Thin liquidity.
- Market orders.
- Fast price movement.
- News-driven volatility.
- Premarket or after-hours trading.
- Large order size compared with available shares.
- Stop orders triggering during a fast move.

Slippage can be small and normal, or it can be large enough to change the quality of the trade.

## Example Scenario

A trader wants to buy a breakout near $3.00. The stock is moving fast, and the trader sends a market order. The order fills at $3.12.

Now the trade is not the same as the planned $3.00 entry.

A trader should ask:

- Was the spread wide?
- Did the order type fit the conditions?
- Was there enough liquidity at $3.00?
- Did the entry fill make the risk too wide?
- Did I chase because the candle was moving fast?

That review matters because the trade may have failed partly because of execution, not only because of the idea.

## Common Mistakes

Common slippage mistakes include:

- Using market orders in thin stocks.
- Ignoring spread before entry.
- Trading too much size for available liquidity.
- Assuming stop orders will always fill near the stop price.
- Reviewing planned price instead of actual fill price.
- Chasing fast candles with no price control.
- Ignoring slippage when calculating risk and reward.

Slippage is not just a broker issue. It is often connected to trade selection, order type, liquidity, and timing.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

Slippage review can help traders separate setup quality from execution quality.

Ask:

- What price did I expect?
- What price did I actually fill at?
- How much slippage occurred?
- Was the spread wide when I entered or exited?
- Did I use a market order or limit order?
- Was my size too large for the liquidity?
- Did slippage change the risk of the trade?

Trader Intelligence is being built to help traders review executions, entries, exits, and repeated mistakes. Slippage review can show whether poor fills are quietly affecting results.

## FAQ

### What is trading slippage?

Trading slippage is the difference between the price a trader expected and the actual price where the order filled.

### Why does slippage happen?

Slippage can happen because of fast price movement, wide spreads, low liquidity, market orders, or limited shares at the expected price.

### Is slippage always bad?

Slippage is usually discussed as negative, but an order can sometimes fill better than expected. The main issue is that actual fills may differ from the plan.

### How can traders reduce slippage?

Traders often review order type, spread, liquidity, position size, and whether the stock is moving too quickly for clean execution.

### Can slippage happen with stop orders?

Yes. If price moves quickly through a stop level, the final fill may be worse than the stop price.

### How should traders review slippage?

Review expected price, actual fill price, spread, order type, liquidity, size, and whether slippage changed the trade's planned risk.
