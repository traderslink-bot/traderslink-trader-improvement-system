# Wedge Pattern

A wedge pattern is a chart pattern where price compresses between two sloping trendlines. Traders watch wedge patterns because they can show tightening price action, slowing momentum, and a possible break from the compression area.

A wedge does not guarantee a breakout or breakdown. It only shows that price is narrowing. The direction, volume, context, and reaction around the break still matter.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They see compression and assume a big move has to happen right away, but wedges can fail, fake out, or keep grinding longer than expected.

## What The Pattern Looks Like

A wedge pattern usually forms when price makes a series of highs and lows that move closer together.

There are two common wedge types:

- Falling wedge pattern.
- Rising wedge pattern.

A falling wedge slopes downward as price compresses. A rising wedge slopes upward as price compresses.

The important feature is narrowing price action. The range gets tighter as buyers and sellers fight for control.

Traders often draw two trendlines around the price action:

- One along the highs.
- One along the lows.

The break of one side of the wedge is what traders usually watch.

## Why Traders Watch It

Traders watch wedge patterns because compression can create a clear area to study.

If price has been narrowing, a break above or below the wedge may attract attention. Traders may watch whether volume expands, whether the break holds, and whether price has room to move after leaving the wedge.

Wedges can help traders review:

- Whether price is compressing cleanly.
- Whether volume is fading during compression.
- Whether the break has follow-through.
- Whether support or resistance is nearby.
- Whether the trade has a clear invalidation area.

The wedge gives structure. It does not give certainty.

## What Can Go Wrong

Wedges can produce false breaks.

Price may break above the upper trendline, pull in buyers, and then fall back into the wedge. Price may also break below the lower trendline and then reclaim.

Other problems include:

- The wedge is drawn too loosely.
- The pattern is too obvious and crowded.
- Volume does not support the break.
- The break happens directly into resistance.
- Liquidity is too thin.
- The trader enters before a clear plan exists.

A wedge can look clean after the fact but feel messy in real time.

## Volume And Context

Volume can help traders review whether the wedge is meaningful.

In some wedge patterns, volume may decline as the range tightens. Then traders may watch for volume expansion during the break.

But volume is not a guarantee. High volume can still fail. Low volume can still move if the stock is thin or if market conditions change.

Context matters. A wedge after a strong move may have a different meaning than a wedge after a long downtrend. A wedge near major resistance may behave differently than a wedge breaking into open space.

## Small-Cap Considerations

Small-cap wedge patterns can be noisy.

Low floats, wide spreads, halts, scanner activity, and sudden news can make a wedge break less reliable. A small-cap stock can break a wedge, fail, reclaim, and reverse quickly.

Small-cap traders should review:

- Spread and liquidity.
- Volume quality.
- Catalyst strength.
- Prior resistance.
- Filing risk.
- Whether the wedge forms during active trading or thin volume.

The pattern should be reviewed with the full trading environment.

## How To Review Trades Involving This Pattern

A wedge trade review should focus on compression, entry timing, and breakout quality.

Useful journal questions include:

- Was the wedge clearly formed before entry?
- Did I enter inside the wedge or after the break?
- Did volume support the break?
- Was there nearby resistance or support?
- Where was the invalidation level?
- Did the break hold or fail quickly?
- Did I chase after the wedge already broke?
- Did I exit when the wedge setup failed?

These questions help separate a planned wedge trade from a rushed chase.

## FAQ

### What is a wedge pattern?

A wedge pattern is a chart pattern where price compresses between two sloping trendlines, creating a narrowing range.

### What are the main types of wedge patterns?

The two common types are falling wedge patterns and rising wedge patterns.

### Is a wedge pattern bullish or bearish?

It depends on the wedge type, trend, breakout direction, volume, and context. No wedge pattern is automatically bullish or bearish in every situation.

### Can wedge patterns fail?

Yes. Wedges can break out, fail, reclaim, or continue compressing longer than expected.

### Why does volume matter in wedge patterns?

Volume can help traders review whether the break has participation, but it does not guarantee follow-through.

### How should I journal wedge trades?

Track the wedge structure, entry timing, breakout direction, volume, invalidation level, and whether price held after the break.
