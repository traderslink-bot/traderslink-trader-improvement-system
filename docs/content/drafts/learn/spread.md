# Bid Ask Spread: Why the Spread Matters in Trading

The bid ask spread is the difference between the highest price buyers are bidding and the lowest price sellers are asking. Traders watch the spread because it affects entry price, exit price, slippage, and real trade cost.

A chart may look clean, but a wide spread can make the trade much harder to execute well.

## Quick Definition

The bid ask spread is the gap between the bid price and the ask price.

- The bid is the highest price someone is currently willing to pay.
- The ask is the lowest price someone is currently willing to sell for.
- The spread is the difference between those two prices.

For example, if the bid is $2.00 and the ask is $2.05, the spread is $0.05.

## Why It Matters To Traders

The spread matters because it can affect the trade before price even moves.

If a trader buys at the ask and immediately needs to sell at the bid, a wide spread can create an instant disadvantage. On low-priced stocks, even a few cents can be a large percentage move.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They see a stock moving fast and enter without checking whether the spread is reasonable for the trade plan.

## How It Works

The spread is usually tighter when a stock has strong liquidity and many active buyers and sellers.

The spread is often wider when:

- The stock is thinly traded.
- The float is low.
- The stock is moving fast.
- The market is premarket or after-hours.
- News just hit.
- Liquidity is uneven.
- There is limited size on the bid or ask.

A tight spread can make execution easier. A wide spread can increase slippage and make stops harder to manage.

## Example Scenario

A stock is trading around $1.00. The chart shows a possible breakout, but the quote shows:

- Bid: $0.97
- Ask: $1.05

That is an $0.08 spread. On a $1.00 stock, that is large.

A trader may ask:

- Can I enter and exit cleanly?
- Is the spread too wide for my planned risk?
- Would a market order fill badly?
- Is there enough size on the bid and ask?
- Am I ignoring execution risk because the chart looks exciting?

The setup may still move, but the spread changes the trade quality.

## Common Mistakes

Common spread mistakes include:

- Using market orders in wide-spread stocks.
- Ignoring the spread in premarket.
- Trading too much size for the available liquidity.
- Thinking the last price is the same as a real fill price.
- Entering a trade where the spread is larger than the planned risk.
- Forgetting that spreads can widen during volatility.
- Reviewing only the chart and not the execution.

A wide spread can turn a decent chart idea into a poor execution setup.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

Spread review helps traders understand whether they lost because of trade idea, execution, or both.

Ask:

- What was the spread when I entered?
- Was the spread acceptable for the stock price and trade plan?
- Did I use a market order or limit order?
- Did the spread create immediate slippage?
- Was the exit harder because the bid disappeared?
- Did the spread widen when volatility increased?
- Do I repeatedly struggle in wide-spread stocks?

Trader Intelligence is being built to help traders review executions and trade-management behavior. Spread context can help explain why a trade felt difficult even if the chart looked clean.

## FAQ

### What is the bid ask spread?

The bid ask spread is the difference between the highest price buyers are bidding and the lowest price sellers are asking.

### Why does the spread matter in trading?

The spread affects execution cost, entry quality, exit quality, and slippage.

### Is a wide spread bad?

A wide spread can make a trade harder to execute and can increase risk, especially for active traders using larger size.

### What causes wide spreads?

Wide spreads often happen when liquidity is thin, volatility is high, trading is outside regular hours, or there are fewer active buyers and sellers.

### Should traders use market orders with wide spreads?

Market orders can fill poorly in wide-spread stocks. Traders often review whether a limit order would have controlled execution better.

### How can traders review spread problems?

Review the bid, ask, spread, order type, fill price, slippage, and whether the spread was reasonable for the trade plan.
