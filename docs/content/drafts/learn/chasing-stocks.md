# Chasing Stocks

Chasing stocks means entering after a move has already become extended, usually because the trader feels pressure to get in before the move goes farther. It often overlaps with FOMO trading, overtrading, and emotional decision-making.

A stock can be strong and still be a bad chase. The issue is not only whether the stock is moving. The issue is whether the entry still has a clear plan, realistic risk, and enough room before resistance.

This is where traders can get into trouble. The candle looks exciting, but the trade may already be late.

## Quick Definition

Chasing stocks means buying or shorting after price has already moved significantly without waiting for a planned entry, pullback, setup, or risk area.

Chasing can look like:

- Buying after several large green candles.
- Entering near high of day without a plan.
- Shorting after a sharp breakdown without checking support.
- Jumping into scanner alerts too late.
- Entering because chat or social media is excited.
- Ignoring spread, slippage, and nearby resistance.
- Taking a trade because the trader missed the cleaner entry.

Chasing is not the same as trading momentum. Momentum trading can be planned. Chasing is usually rushed.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Chasing matters because late entries often create poor risk-to-reward.

When price is extended, the risk area may be far away. The trader may need a wide stop, but they may not want to accept that risk. If price pulls back normally, the trader can panic. If price reverses, the trader may hold too long because they entered emotionally.

Chasing can also lead to:

- Poor fills.
- Wide spreads.
- Slippage.
- Buying into resistance.
- Shorting into support.
- Oversized trades.
- Revenge entries after missing a move.
- Repeated losses from low-quality setups.

The problem is not one chase. The bigger problem is when chasing becomes a repeated habit.

## How It Works

Chasing often starts when a stock moves quickly.

The trader sees the move and feels urgency. Instead of waiting for a planned setup, they enter because they are afraid the move will continue without them.

A better review process asks:

- Where was the clean entry?
- Did I miss it?
- Is there still a setup now?
- Where is the risk?
- Is resistance too close?
- Is volume still supporting the move?
- Am I entering because of a plan or because I feel late?

Sometimes the best trade is no trade. Missing a move is not the same as needing to chase the next candle.

## Example Scenario

Imagine a stock breaks above $2.00 and runs to $2.80 on heavy volume. The clean breakout entry was near $2.05, but the trader hesitated.

At $2.78, the stock hits another scanner alert. The trader buys because it feels like the stock could go to $3.00.

But resistance is near $2.90. The spread is wide. The stock is already extended. When price pulls back to $2.50, the trader is immediately uncomfortable.

The issue is not that the stock had momentum. The issue is that the entry came after the clean risk area was gone.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is assuming that a strong stock is always worth entering. Strength matters, but entry location matters too.

Another mistake is ignoring distance from support. If the nearest real risk area is too far away, the trade may be harder to manage.

Traders also make mistakes when they chase because they missed the first move. Missing a trade can create emotional pressure.

Another mistake is using large size on a late entry. A late entry usually deserves more caution, not more size.

A final mistake is refusing to label the trade honestly in the journal. If the trade was a chase, the journal should say so.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

Chasing behavior should be reviewed directly because it is often repeated.

Useful journal questions include:

- Was my entry late relative to the setup?
- Did I miss the cleaner entry?
- Did I enter because of FOMO?
- Was price already extended?
- Was there nearby resistance or support?
- Did I define risk before entering?
- Did spread or slippage affect the trade?
- Would I take the same entry again if the stock was not moving fast?

A trading journal can help reveal whether chasing happens after missed moves, losses, scanner alerts, or chatroom excitement.

## FAQ

### What does chasing stocks mean?

Chasing stocks means entering after a move is already extended, often because the trader feels pressure to avoid missing the move.

### Is chasing always bad?

Not every late entry loses, but chasing often creates poor risk-to-reward and emotional trade management.

### Is chasing the same as momentum trading?

No. Momentum trading can be planned with clear risk and structure. Chasing is usually rushed and emotional.

### Why do traders chase stocks?

Traders chase because of FOMO, missed entries, scanner alerts, social excitement, revenge trading, or fear that the stock will keep moving without them.

### How can I tell if I chased a trade?

Review whether the entry was late, whether risk was defined, whether price was extended, and whether you entered because of urgency rather than a plan.

### How should I journal chasing stocks?

Track the original clean entry, your actual entry, distance from support or resistance, emotional trigger, risk plan, and whether the trade fit your rules.
