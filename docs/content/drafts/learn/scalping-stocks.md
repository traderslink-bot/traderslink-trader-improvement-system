# Scalping Stocks: What Traders Should Know

Scalping stocks is a short-term trading style where traders try to capture small price moves, often quickly. A scalp trade may last seconds or minutes, and the focus is usually on speed, execution, liquidity, and tight risk.

Scalping can look simple because the target is small. In practice, small mistakes can matter a lot. Bad entries, wide spreads, poor fills, and hesitation can turn a quick idea into a messy trade.

## Quick Definition

Stock scalping is a fast trading style built around short-term price movement.

Scalpers often focus on:

- Liquid stocks.
- Tight spreads.
- Fast entries and exits.
- Short holding periods.
- Momentum bursts.
- Level 2 or tape behavior.
- Support and resistance reactions.
- Small but repeatable opportunities.

The goal is not to catch the whole move. The goal is to execute a short-term idea with control.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Scalping matters because it puts pressure on execution.

A swing trader may have time to think. A scalper often does not. The entry, exit, and risk decision must happen quickly. That means the trader needs a clear plan before clicking.

This is where traders can get into trouble. A scalp trade that starts with a tight idea can become a hold, an average down, or a revenge trade if the trader refuses to exit when the quick idea fails.

## How Scalping Works

A scalp trade usually starts with a specific short-term reason.

Examples may include:

- A breakout through a nearby level.
- A reclaim of a key area.
- A quick pullback into support.
- A momentum push with strong volume.
- A reaction to bid or ask strength.
- A bounce after a fast selloff.

The trade usually needs a clear invalidation area. If the reason for the scalp disappears, the trade should not become something else without a new plan.

## Example Scenario

A stock breaks above high of day with strong volume. A scalper enters for a quick continuation move. The stock pops briefly, then immediately falls back under the breakout level.

A disciplined scalp review might ask:

- Was the entry late?
- Did the breakout hold?
- Was the spread manageable?
- Did I exit when the idea failed?
- Did I turn a scalp into a hold?
- Did I take the trade because of a plan or because the candle looked exciting?

Scalping is fast, but the review should still be detailed.

## Common Mistakes

Common scalping mistakes include:

- Entering too late.
- Ignoring spreads.
- Over-sizing for a small move.
- Holding after the scalp idea fails.
- Averaging down a quick trade.
- Overtrading because each trade feels small.
- Chasing every momentum burst.
- Failing to account for commissions, slippage, or poor fills.

The small target does not make the trade automatically low risk.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

After a scalp trade, review execution quality carefully.

Ask:

- What was the exact reason for the scalp?
- Was the entry clean or late?
- Was the spread tight enough?
- Did I exit quickly when the idea failed?
- Did I hold longer than planned?
- Did I take too many scalp trades in one session?
- Were the wins worth the risk and effort?

Scalping performance often depends on small execution details, so journaling matters.

## FAQ

### What is scalping stocks?

Scalping stocks is a short-term trading style where traders try to capture small price moves quickly, often within seconds or minutes.

### Is scalping the same as day trading?

Scalping is a type of day trading, but not all day trading is scalping. Scalping focuses on shorter holding periods and smaller moves.

### Is stock scalping risky?

Yes. Scalping can be risky because execution speed, spreads, slippage, and position size can affect the trade quickly.

### What do scalpers watch?

Scalpers often watch volume, spreads, support and resistance, momentum, Level 2, time and sales, and short-term price reactions.

### Can scalping lead to overtrading?

Yes. Because scalp trades are quick, traders may take too many low-quality trades if they are not disciplined.

### Should I journal scalp trades?

Yes. Journaling scalp trades helps review entry timing, exit timing, spreads, slippage, and whether the trader followed the quick trade plan.
