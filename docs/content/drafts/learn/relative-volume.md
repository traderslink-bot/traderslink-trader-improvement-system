# Relative Volume Explained

Relative volume compares a stock's current volume to its normal volume. Traders use relative volume to identify unusual activity, especially in small-cap, penny stock, and momentum trading.

High relative volume can show that a stock is receiving more attention than usual. It does not guarantee a good trade, but it can help traders understand when activity is abnormal.

## Quick Definition

Relative volume, often shortened to RVOL, measures current volume compared with average volume over a previous period.

For example, if a stock usually trades 1 million shares by a certain time of day but trades 5 million shares today by the same time, its relative volume is much higher than normal.

The exact calculation can vary by platform, but the idea is the same: compare today's activity to normal activity.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Volume tells traders how much activity is happening. Relative volume tells traders whether that activity is unusual.

This matters because unusual volume can appear when:

- News is released.
- SEC filings are published.
- A stock appears on scanners.
- A sector gains attention.
- A breakout or breakdown attracts traders.
- Short-term momentum increases.

High relative volume can help traders find stocks that are active, but it should be reviewed with context.

## How Relative Volume Works

Relative volume compares current activity to a baseline.

That baseline may use average daily volume, average volume at the same time of day, or another platform-specific formula. Because calculations can differ, RVOL numbers may not match exactly across scanners or data providers.

The main idea is to ask:

- Is today's volume normal?
- Is today's volume unusually high?
- Is volume increasing or fading?
- Is the volume supported by a real catalyst?
- Is there enough liquidity to trade safely?

## Example Scenario

A small-cap stock usually trades 200,000 shares in a full day. Today it trades 10 million shares before noon after a press release.

That is unusual activity. A trader may review:

- What caused the volume?
- Is the news meaningful?
- Is the move holding key levels?
- Is the spread manageable?
- Is the stock low float?
- Is there dilution risk?
- Is volume still increasing or fading?

High RVOL is a signal of attention, not a complete trading plan.

## Common Mistakes

Common relative volume mistakes include:

- Treating high RVOL as an automatic buy signal.
- Ignoring why volume is high.
- Ignoring float and liquidity.
- Chasing after the move is already extended.
- Comparing RVOL numbers across platforms without understanding calculation differences.
- Ignoring volume fade after the initial spike.
- Ignoring dilution or filing risk in small caps.

Relative volume is useful, but it needs context.

## How To Review This In Your Trading Journal

After trading a high-relative-volume stock, review how volume affected the trade.

Ask:

- Was relative volume part of the reason for the trade?
- What caused the unusual activity?
- Did volume continue after entry or fade?
- Did I chase because volume was high?
- Did liquidity affect execution?
- Did the stock hold key levels with volume?
- Did I exit when volume weakened?
- Did similar RVOL trades create repeated mistakes?

Volume context can make trade review more specific.

## FAQ

### What is relative volume?

Relative volume compares a stock's current trading volume to its normal or average volume. It helps traders see when activity is unusually high or low.

### What does high relative volume mean?

High relative volume means a stock is trading much more volume than usual. It can show unusual attention, but it does not guarantee a profitable trade.

### Is RVOL the same on every platform?

No. Different scanners and data providers may calculate relative volume differently, especially around time-of-day comparisons or average volume windows.

### Why do small-cap traders watch relative volume?

Small-cap traders watch relative volume because unusual activity can appear when a stock has news, filings, sector momentum, or sudden trader attention.

### Can high RVOL be risky?

Yes. High relative volume can attract fast moves, but it can also attract chasing, volatility, halts, wide spreads, and sharp reversals.

### Should I track relative volume in my journal?

Yes, if volume was part of your trade idea. Tracking RVOL can help you review whether unusual volume supported the trade or caused you to chase.

## Conclusion

Relative volume helps traders identify unusual stock activity. It can be especially useful for small-cap and momentum traders, but it should always be reviewed with catalyst quality, float, liquidity, chart context, and risk.

If you trade volume-driven stocks, reviewing RVOL after the trade can help you understand whether the activity supported your plan or pulled you into a reactive decision.
